CASE STUDY · §03 / 03

A mobile learning product 10,000 students actually returned to on Tuesday.

Innova Schools is one of Latin America's largest private K-12 networks. They needed a mobile companion product their students would use voluntarily outside of class hours — something that lived next to TikTok on the home screen and won at least a few of those battles. I was the product designer. We shipped to 10,000+ learners and held a 26% D30 retention rate in a category where most edtech apps die at 8%.

§ 01 — PROBLEM

EdTech that "works" still loses to literally anything else on a phone.

Innova had a captive in-class product. Outside class, they had nothing — and the parent base was asking for something. The first instinct was to build a homework tracker. The data on every similar product I could find said this would die at D7. Students don't want more school in their pockets. They want progress.

The hard truth I had to walk leadership through: the product had to earn its opens on the same terms as a game or a social app. That meant rewarding the act of opening, surfacing perceived growth in seconds, and never punishing a missed day. School can punish. The app couldn't.

CATEGORY BASELINE

Most LATAM mobile EdTech apps shipping in 2020 saw D30 retention between 6–10% (internal benchmark across 14 comparable apps). The category had trained students to expect a chore.

OUR TARGET

20% D30. The PM and I pre-committed to killing the product if we couldn't hold it for 90 days. We hit 26% and held. Killing the product was off the table.

Why this framing mattered

Innova's leadership wanted "an app for the students". My contribution at the kick-off was reframing this as "a product the students will choose to open". Same words, different metric. The whole project hinged on this — every feature we cut or kept was tested against "would a 14-year-old voluntarily open this on a Sunday?".

Game designers have been solving "make people come back tomorrow" for decades. EdTech has been solving "make people learn the material". These are different problems with different toolkits. The breakthrough for me was admitting we needed the game-design toolkit first — because if no one opens the app, the learning toolkit is irrelevant.

This case-study is what made me a retention designer. Everything I do now traces back to it.

§ 02 — RESEARCH

I asked 30 students what they did on Sunday night.

Not "what they liked about school". Not "how they preferred to study". I asked them to walk me through the apps on their home screen, in order, and tell me when they'd last opened each one. The pattern that emerged was the entire thesis of the product:

  • Students opened apps that showed them they'd grown — streaks, levels, follower counts.
  • They avoided apps that made them feel behind — calendar reminders, unfinished tasks.
  • The most-used app on every device was the one that felt the cheapest to open — under 3 seconds to a reward.
FIG. 1 · two-loop retention map · what we built v3 · annotated

Two loops, one feeding the other. Loop A earns the daily return. Loop B re-engages students who fell out of Loop A — even a missed week ended with a Friday recap card celebrating whatever did happen. The two loops compound instead of competing.

We also discovered (and this surprised the team) that students who shared their week recap with a parent were 3.4× more likely to be on D30. Parent visibility was a retention multiplier — and it cost nothing to add. †

† This was the unexpected finding. The students didn't want their parents checking on them — but they did want to show off something they'd earned. The framing matters: pride loop, not surveillance loop.
§ 03 — THE SYSTEM

Four design moves. One product principle.

The product principle: never make a student feel behind. Behind any feature that violated this, no matter how pedagogically sound, we cut. This principle alone killed half the original product spec — and the result is the half that shipped.

FIG. 2 · onboarding spine 7 screens · ~60s · ends in xp

Move 1 — Earn the first session in 90 seconds

Old plan: 14 onboarding screens, ending on a subject-picker. New plan: 7 screens, ending with a completed 90-second card, +25 XP, and a streak of 1. Mastery and full subject picking happen after the first reward — never before.

Move 2 — Push notifications never ask for a task

Most EdTech push reads "you have 3 assignments due". We made a hard rule: our push messages only celebrate or invite, never demand. "Tu racha de 4 días te espera 👋" instead of "completa tu lección de hoy". Push CTR went from 6% to 17% in week one.

CELEBRATE · "Tu racha llegó a 7 días — sigues fuerte."

INVITE · "Tu tarjeta de hoy te espera (90s)."

CURIOUS · "¿Sabías que mejoraste 12% en historia esta semana?"

Banned categories: anything starting with "completa", "no olvides", or any fear-of-missing-out language. We tested these — they got opens but tanked retention.

Move 3 — Make growth visible at every scale

The daily card produces XP (micro). XP fills a subject-level bar (meso). Subject bars feed into a "this month you grew most in…" card on the home screen (macro). Three time-scales of visible progress, all reinforcing each other. The student always has something to look at that's moved since the last open.

Move 4 — Streaks that forgive

Hardcore streaks are hostile. We built in a "freeze day" — every Sunday, the streak holds even if the student doesn't open the app. Missed-day messaging read "tu racha sigue en pie" instead of "perdiste tu racha". This was the single biggest factor in retention past day 14, where the brittle-streak apps usually die.

"Mom, mira lo que aprendí esta semana." — Student, 13, beta · the line that closed the deal internally

The lesson I took out of this and into every project since: retention is a system, not a feature. The streak alone wouldn't have done it. The push copy alone wouldn't have done it. The growth bar alone wouldn't have done it. The four moves together, reinforcing one another — that's the whole thing. Take one away and retention collapses.

What I'd do differently

  • Build the parent-side product earlier. The "share with parent" feature was an afterthought, and it ended up being the strongest D14+ lever. It deserved more design time.
  • Instrument before launching. I lost two weeks adding GA events after the fact. Funnels are part of the spec, not the cleanup.
  • Stop calling it homework. Internal language matters; the team kept slipping into "tarea" and the design followed. Once we made "tarjeta" (card) the only allowed noun, the screens improved.
§ 05 — WHAT'S NEXT

The version I'd build now.

Five years later, with what I've learned doing game systems at Edenic Era and B2C ecosystems at Lyly, the version I'd ship is fundamentally a multi-loop product from day one — and probably a small social layer between siblings or classmates. The pride loop with parents was the surprise insight. The next surprise insight is sitting in peer-to-peer growth.
