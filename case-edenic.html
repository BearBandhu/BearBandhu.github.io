§ 01 — PROBLEM

In games, retention is the only metric that matters.

Product designers tend to think of retention as one metric among many. Game designers know it's the metric — because if no one plays your game on day two, no other metric exists. This honesty about what matters is, in my opinion, what makes game designers the world's best retention designers. Every part of the craft is tuned to "did the player come back?" — and the question is asked ruthlessly, daily, in playtests.

At Edenic Era, my job is to design the gameplay systems so that the answer is yes. Not the cinematics. Not the art. The systems — the loop, the economy, the failure-recovery — the load-bearing structure that determines whether a player wakes up the next day thinking about us.

GAME DESIGN ANSWER

The second session is the most important session in the game. Everything is designed around it: the first session ends on an unresolved-but-not-frustrating beat, the offline state creates pull, the return rewards the right action.

PRODUCT DESIGN PARALLEL

Most products optimize the first session (onboarding) and the long-tail (engagement). They under-design the second session. The second session is where retention is decided. The same is true in product. Steal this.

§ 02 — RESEARCH / METHOD

Playtests, balance tables, and a brutally honest spreadsheet.

The game designer's research toolkit is mostly two things: playtests and balance spreadsheets. We run small playtests early and often — 4 to 6 players, one hour, observed in person where possible. The questions are simple. Did they come back? What did they remember? Where did they get bored?

The balance spreadsheet does the math the playtests can't. We model the economy — every resource, every cost, every gain — across 60 simulated player sessions and look for the moments where the curves go wrong. A bored player is almost always a player on the wrong side of a balance curve.

FIG. 1 · core loop · with retention triggers v8 · annotated

The labels on the loop are the heart of the work: each transition has a phrase the player should silently be thinking. If a playtester doesn't say something close to "I earned this" when finishing a challenge, the reward isn't reading. We adjust the animation, the audio, the timing — small craft moves — until the phrase shows up unprompted in feedback. †

† This is the technique most worth stealing from game design. Every transition in a product is a moment the user is silently narrating. Design the narration, not just the screen.
§ 03 — THE SYSTEM

End every session on "almost".

The single highest-leverage retention design move in the game is how the first session ends. We end it on a hook — the player is 80% of the way to a meaningful upgrade, not 100%. The cliffhanger is small, specific, and resolvable in the first 3 minutes of session 2.

The daily-shape system

The other system I'm proudest of is what we call the daily shape. Every calendar day, the game proposes a shape for the player's session — three short activities, a longer one, and a "discovery" slot. The shapes vary day to day so the game never feels like a checklist. The shape is suggestion, not demand — players can ignore it without penalty.

▒ Each day's shape is the default — players choose. The system suggests; it never demands.

The standard mistake in retention design is to over-reward and under-forgive. Streak apps that punish missed days produce higher 7-day metrics and lower 30-day metrics, because the punishment is the moment the user uninstalls.

At Edenic Era we built forgiveness into the calendar from the start. Sunday is a free day. Streaks freeze on travel. Missed days roll into a "catch up" rather than a loss. The game forgives, so the player stays.

Balance is design

An economy that's too generous bores players. An economy that's too stingy frustrates them. The space between is narrow, and the only way to find it is to model the math. I maintain a 14-tab spreadsheet of every economy curve — and yes, the spreadsheet is more of the work than the screens. Most products have an economy whether they admit it or not. Time spent, attention invested, social capital earned — these are resources flowing through a system. Designing without modeling that system is hoping.

Edenic Era is not done. What's done is the thinking I want to bring to my next product role. Game designers have decades of hard-won retention craft. Most product teams aren't using any of it. I'd like to be the bridge.

What I'm taking with me

  • The second session is the metric. Design every onboarding to end on a hook, not a completion.
  • Model the economy. Even non-game products have one. Build the spreadsheet.
  • Forgive more than you reward. Punishment creates short-term metrics and long-term churn.
  • Design the player's internal narration. Every transition should have a phrase the user is silently saying. Make sure it's the right one.
