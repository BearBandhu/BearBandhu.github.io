CASE STUDY · §02 / 03

Designing one B2C mobile app inside a three-app ecosystem, with one team of eight.

Lyly Interactive is a B2C mobile studio with a three-product ecosystem aimed at adjacent audiences. I led product design and helped run the program — eight people, Agile sprints, a constant tension between shipping standalone MVPs and shipping something that compounded across the three apps. I designed the shared system that let us do both.

§ 01 — PROBLEM

Three apps. One team. Diverging visual languages.

When I joined Lyly, three B2C mobile products were in different states of build-out. Each had its own designer (in rotation) and its own visual direction. The three products targeted overlapping audiences but spoke in three different voices. Engineering was paying the tax in duplicated code and decisions; users who used more than one app were paying the tax in cognitive whiplash.

The org's reflex was to merge the apps. We argued — and the data agreed — that merging would dilute each product's specific value proposition. The right move was the opposite: keep the apps separate, unify the system underneath.

BEFORE

Each app had its own type scale, color palette, component implementations, and onboarding patterns. Sprint velocity was bottlenecked by design handoff — eng was waiting on three separate component decisions in parallel.

AFTER

One shared design system. One library of components in code. Each app expresses itself through a small set of brand tokens. App #3 went from spec to MVP in 6 weeks because it inherited everything.

§ 02 — RESEARCH

I audited the three codebases before I audited the design files.

The most useful research artifact in this project was a spreadsheet of every component used across the three apps, with the actual delta noted. Buttons that looked different but were 95% the same. Modals that were structurally identical with different padding. A list of about 60 components that, with discipline, could become 20.

FIG. 1 · component consolidation · 60 → 20 audit · v2

The hardest part of the consolidation wasn't the technical work — it was the social work. Each app's previous designer had reasons for each variant. Some were good reasons (a specific accessibility constraint), most were not (it was Tuesday). I spent two weeks running consolidation reviews with engineers, not designers, because the engineers were the ones who knew where the variants were actually different and where they only looked different. †

† This is the move I'd recommend to any designer joining a multi-product team: audit with the engineers first, the designers second. Engineers will tell you the truth about how often a variant is actually used.
"I shipped the third app's settings screen in a day. A year ago it would have been a sprint." — Engineer, after the system went live · week 9
§ 03 — THE SYSTEM

Tokens behind. Patterns in front.

The unfashionable opinion I held throughout this project: we don't need a massive token system. We need a small set of patterns. Most design systems over-build tokens (the syntax) and under-build patterns (the sentences). At Lyly's scale — three apps, eight people — patterns were the higher-leverage investment.

The three layers

LAYER 1 · Brand tokens. Per-app: color, type face, default radius. About 18 tokens, no more.

LAYER 2 · Shared components. The 20 components from the audit. Built once in code, themed by the tokens.

LAYER 3 · Patterns. The high-leverage layer. Onboarding spine, empty states, payment sheet, settings shape. Each pattern is a recipe of components plus the rule for how they compose.

FIG. 2 · pattern spec · onboarding spine (shared) v4 final

The shared onboarding spine

All three apps now use the same four-screen onboarding rhythm: welcome → intent → quick win → commit. The screens look different per app — different brand, different copy, different micro-interactions. But the rhythm is identical, and the metrics are comparable across apps. We can A/B test changes in one app and lift them into the others with high confidence they'll hold.

The quick-win screen does one thing: produce a small, real, visible reward within 90 seconds of app open. The constraint forces every PM into a useful conversation: what is the one thing your user can experience that's actually good in the first 90 seconds?

App 1 (B2C, audio): "Hear your first 30-second clip + save it."
App 2 (B2C, social): "See three friends already here + send one a wave."
App 3 (B2C, content): "Read one thing, get one recommendation, save both."

How handoff changed

Before: design files contained pixel-perfect mocks of every screen, per app. After: design files contain tokens + a list of patterns to compose. Engineers stopped asking "what does this button look like" because they already knew. The conversation moved up a level: what's the right pattern here? — which is the conversation worth having anyway.

§ 04 — OUTCOME

The system compounded.

−57%

−67%

~2×
tickets closed / week, post-system

The thing I'm proudest of: the system didn't slow down product work. Most design-system efforts produce a beautiful library and a delivery slowdown. We built ours by extracting from the products that were already shipping, never ahead of them. By the time the system was "done", it was already in use.

What I'd do differently

  • Document the patterns sooner. The components were clear; the patterns lived in my head for two months too long. New designers joining couldn't ramp without them.
  • Build the third app's MVP first. If I'd known what the third app would teach us, I'd have prototyped its onboarding before finalizing the spine. We retrofitted instead, which cost two weeks.
  • More cross-app user research. We had great per-app insight; we had almost no insight into the user who touched two apps. That's the most valuable user we had.
§ 05 — WHAT'S NEXT

The cross-app pattern I didn't get to ship.

The natural next step at Lyly is a cross-app identity — the user signs in once, their progress and preferences travel. We sketched it. It didn't ship before my engagement ended. The hardest part is not the auth (solved) — it's the design of the cross-app surface: what do you show a user who has interesting state in two of your three apps? That's the question I'd love to come back to.
